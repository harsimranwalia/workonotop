# WorkOnTap Phase 1: handoff

This note says what Phase 1 covers, how each promise in the contract was checked, what could only be attempted in the development environment, and what you need to supply before the system can run for customers. It describes one commit: **e529a5d 2026-10-17**. Any later change to the code makes the table in section 3 out of date; run the suite again (section 2) to refresh it.

Nothing described here ran against production. Production is not part of this build.

## 1. What this is

Phase 1 is the quote-to-job core of the platform:

- A customer books online, one time or on a schedule, at the price the pricing rules produce. The card is held when the booking is made. It is charged once for a visit, after the visit is done and the customer has approved it (or, at the first hourly sweep after the job has waited 24 hours unchanged with no answer, unless the payment has failed or a dispute is open or under review). A schedule holds the card for its first visit only, and each later visit is charged to that card once it is approved, by the customer or by the 24-hour sweep, and never before that visit's own date. The app's own payment step always holds the amount; a payment that was taken by automatic capture in the last 24 hours, for exactly the booking's price, by the customer the booking is for, and that names no booking of its own, is accepted too, and recorded as paid.
- A job that cannot be priced becomes a quote request. An admin sees the jobs of customers who have an account in the Jobs list (the newest 50), and every job still waiting for a provider or an acceptance in the queue; a job for a customer with no account is never in the Jobs list; it is in the queue only while it waits for a provider or an acceptance, and after that an admin finds it only among that provider's jobs on the Users page (section 6). An admin books on a customer's behalf (the customer is sent the quote and a link to add a card), and assigns a job or offers it to the marketplace.
- A provider keeps a crew list and puts cleaners on a job. Nothing makes the job's link on its own: the provider (or an admin) presses "Create link" once the job has a crew, and each cleaner on the crew whose number has passed its check is texted the link, provided the job is open, the job's provider is active, the app's public address is set and the daily text limits (8 to one number, 200 for one provider, 500 in all) are not used up; a text that is refused is recorded in `notice_attempts` and not sent. A cleaner added later, or whose number passes its check later, is texted too while the link is on.
- A cleaner types their number, is texted a six-digit code (good for 10 minutes, 5 tries), enters it, and checks in everyone who showed up. The check-in opens an hour before the first slot of the job's first day and does not close while the job is open, so a late arrival can still check in. Before photos are taken, and the job is started from the link with a cleaner checked in and the phone's location recorded (the provider's own Start Job button can also start a confirmed job that has a before photo, with no check-in and no location: the one departure in section 3, which section 7 asks about). A countdown shares what is left of the quoted labour among the cleaners who are checked in at that moment, and divides it again when the crew changes.
- On-site reports reach the admin queue, and extra items are charged only after an admin approves them.
- The code sends the texts and e-mails the contract lists (section 4 says what could and could not be checked here), and the nine defects the contract names were fixed.

The contract lists twelve things by which "we'll know it worked", W1 to W12. Five of them are hard gates: W2, W5, W9, W11 and W12. W11 is the nine named defects, D1 to D9. The automated suite checks them: 1,994 cases in 72 files under `e2e/`. A map in `e2e/acceptance/contract.js` says which cases prove which sentence of the contract, and `npm run acceptance` reads a run of the suite against that map and says, for each item, whether the suite shows it.

Section 3 is what the suite shows. Section 4 is what is known only from what the code tries to do. Section 5 is what you supply. Section 6 is what is not built. Section 7 is where the evidence has gaps and the decisions asked of you.

## 2. Bring up the dev environment and run the suite

The development environment is two containers: an app container that runs the Next.js development server on port 3000 against the checkout, and a MySQL 8.4 container (host port 3307, database `workontap_db`) that holds 46 tables and no data: the 33 tables of the live schema and the 13 that the eleven migrations create (step 2). The compose file and the development Dockerfile that build the containers are not committed to the repository, so this is what they hold. The app container is `node:22-alpine` with the checkout mounted at `/app`, `/app/node_modules` and `/app/.next` kept in volumes of their own, the packages installed with `npm ci --ignore-scripts` followed by `node scripts/patch-ts-interface.cjs`, and `npm run dev -- -H 0.0.0.0` as its command. Its environment names the database container in `DB_HOST` (`db`) and sets `DB_USER` (`root`), `DB_PASSWORD` (the database container's root password), `DB_NAME` (`workontap_db`), `STRIPE_COUNTRY` (`CA`) and `STRIPE_CURRENCY` (`cad`). The database container is the `mysql:8.4` image with `MYSQL_DATABASE` set to `workontap_db`, the root password `root123`, and its port 3306 published as 3307. Those two are not yours to choose: 36 of the 72 spec files reach the database themselves, and each spells out `127.0.0.1`, port 3307, user `root`, password `root123` and database `workontap_db` on a line of its own, with no setting to change them, so a different password or port means changing that line in all 36. The app's own `DB_PASSWORD` and the fixture command follow whatever the container has. The app always connects on port 3306: `DB_PORT` is read by the fixture command, `database/schema.js` and `database/seed_live_services.js`; the app and `database/add_missing_columns.mjs` do not read it and always use 3306. The fixture command, which empties every table, runs in the app container and takes that container's `DB_HOST`: it refuses to run unless that is `db`, `localhost`, `127.0.0.1` or `::1` and the database is called `workontap_db`, and it refuses a database in which `users.email`, `service_providers.email`, `bookings.customer_email` or `deletion_requests.email` holds an address outside the reserved test names (`.test`, `.example`, `.invalid`, `.localhost`, `example.com`, `example.net` and `example.org`). It looks at those four columns and no others, and nothing turns the check off. The suite only ever sees synthetic rows.

In order:

1. Start the stack with the checkout mounted into the app container. The app needs `JWT_SECRET` in its environment (for the development server, in `.env.development.local`); without it every customer, provider and admin sign-in answers 500.
2. Build the schema in the empty database, before anything else is loaded. The suite needs 46 tables: the 33 of the live schema (a schema-only dump of the live database; the repository does not hold one) and the 13 that the eleven migrations in `database/migrations/` create. Apply the `.up.sql` files by hand, in the order that section 5 lists. The development database was built this way. `npm run db:init`, which runs `database/schema.js`, is not a substitute for the live schema: it builds 45 tables of its own, it has no `booking_audit_logs`, it lacks 29 columns that the live schema has, it types a job's date (`bookings.job_date` and `invoices.job_date`) as a date where the live schema holds text, and its list of provider statuses leaves out `pending_deletion` and `deleted`. The suite has not been run against a database built from it. The fixture command in the next step loads data only; it builds no schema.
3. Load the synthetic data, before a run and after it (a run writes more than one activity-log row: the pre-flight sign-in and each web sign-in after it add one):

   ```
   docker exec workontap-app npm run db:fixtures
   ```

4. Tell the suite which variables the app has, run it, then read the run against the contract. The suite learns which credentials the app holds only from `E2E_APP_ENV_NAMES`, the comma-separated names (never the values) of the variables the app has set. Without it, three cases are skipped instead of run (the approval with no payment key in `job-reports.spec.js`, the `stripe-complete` case in `ownership.spec.js` and the valid sign-up in `provider-signup-page.spec.js`), the journey case leaves out its approval step, and `npm run acceptance` reads W12, a hard gate, and W8 as NOT MET. One way to read the names from the app container, as the build's own runner does:

   ```
   export E2E_APP_ENV_NAMES=$(docker exec workontap-app node -e 'const{combinedEnv:e}=require("@next/env").loadEnvConfig("/app",true,{info(){},error(){}});console.log(Object.keys(e).filter(k=>e[k]).join(","))')
   npm test
   npm run acceptance
   ```

   `npm test` is Playwright 1.58.2. On a machine with no browser build for that version, run the same command inside the published Playwright 1.58.2 image with the checkout mounted, the host network and `-e E2E_APP_ENV_NAMES`, so that the suite reaches the app at `http://localhost:3000` (`E2E_BASE_URL` changes the address).

If the stack or the fixtures are missing, the suite stops at the start and prints a line that says which. The two you will meet are:

- `app not reachable at <url> after <n> s (last: <problem>)`
- `fixtures not loaded in the database the app reads (fixture sign-in answered <status>); run the fixture command: npm run db:fixtures`

If the app answers but fails the fixture sign-in, the line is `the app failed the fixture sign-in at <url><route> (HTTP <status>); see the app's log` (or `fixture sign-in at <url><route> failed (<code>)` when nothing answers), and it is the app's log to read, not the fixtures to reload.

`npm test -- e2e/<file>.spec.js` runs one file; a full run is long (about two and a quarter hours of test time on the build host, counting the windows that had to be repeated, and about an hour and twenty minutes in a later complete run on another machine), so it is easiest to run it in windows of a few files and to give the acceptance command the union: copy `test-results/baseline-candidate.json` to a name of your own after each window (the next run replaces it), merge the `cases` of the copies into one file of the same shape and pass it with `--results`. `npm run acceptance -- --markdown --label "<commit> <date>"` prints the tables in section 3. It exits 1 when a hard-gate item is not shown, or departs from the contract with no acceptance written into the map. The README has the rest (the fixture rules, the baseline file, the `ATTEMPTED` outcome).

## 3. The twelve items

How to read the table. An item is the clauses of the contract's own sentence, and takes the status of its worst clause. The five count columns count clauses.

- **MET** (Live): requests to the running app and its database, or a real browser, show it.
- **MET (in-process)** (In process): the code that does it was run directly in the test and not through the running app. Where it calls an outside service (the card service), a stand-in took its place, because the development environment has no account with it. The job clock's arithmetic, the role guard, the table of routes, the key that secures every login and the text of the e-mail call no outside service, so there was nothing to stand in for. D4 and D5 carry this label too, but their cases go through the running app: they book through the public checkout, move the booking with the app's own routes and read back what it stored. The label understates them. What none of them does is make the charge, because there is no card account.
- **ATTEMPT-ONLY** (Attempt only): the code was seen to try, and nothing was delivered. Section 4.
- **DEPARTS** (Departs): the build does something different from the contract's words. The difference is written in the map and waits for a decision (section 7). A hard-gate item that departs and has not been accepted in writing makes the acceptance command exit 1.
- **NOT MET** or **NOT RUN** (Not met): a case failed, was skipped, was renamed or deleted, or has no result. A hard-gate item that is NOT MET is a failure, always. NOT RUN appears only in a `--partial` run, is not a verdict, and does not change the exit code.

## 4. Verified only by what the code attempts

Some promises end at a service the development environment has no account with: Twilio (texts), an SMTP server (e-mail) and Stripe (the card). For these the suite shows that the code decides to send, to the right people, once, and that it records the attempt. It cannot show that the service delivers. The cases that go through the running app report `ATTEMPTED (missing: ...)`, never `PASS`, and the acceptance command prints the clause as ATTEMPT-ONLY. It is never counted as met. The cases that call the notice code directly, with a transport that only records what it was asked to send, have no credential to miss and do read `PASS`; they show who would be told, not that anything arrives.

**Texts and e-mails** (W3: the quote and card link; W4: the job link; W8: the alerts for an on-site report; W10: each of the seven kinds, which are a quote requested, a quote and card link sent, a job booked, a job assigned or reassigned, a job claimed, a job link sent, and an on-site report). Every notice claims a row in `notice_attempts` before anything is sent: one row for one person, one message and one channel, holding the kind, the channel, who it was for, the booking and how it ended. The row holds no number, address, name or message text. With no Twilio or SMTP credential the row ends `not_configured` and nothing leaves the machine. The cases read those rows: who was told, on which channel, how many times (once), and that nobody else was. "Once" is the rule for a send that reached Twilio or the mail server: whatever came back, it keeps its place, so a timeout or a carrier error is never answered with a second text. A notice that was never attempted does not keep its place, so the same event writes another row when it comes again: that is so for a notice with no credential, with no usable number or address, to a number that cannot be texted or has not been checked yet, for a provider who is not active, for a link with no public address to carry (`no_origin`), or stopped by one of the spending limits (outcomes `kind_cap`, `number_cap`, `provider_cap`, `platform_cap` and `mail_cap`). The cases are in `notices-routes.spec.js` (the route that raises each notice), `notices-events.spec.js` (who is told for each event), `acceptance-seams.spec.js` (job booked; the alerts for each kind of report) and `acceptance-journey.spec.js` (the link texted to two cleaners and to a late third; the alerts for an added-items report). Of the cases in those four files, `notices-routes.spec.js` (17), `acceptance-seams.spec.js` (6) and `acceptance-journey.spec.js` (3) end `ATTEMPTED`, and so do 3 in `quote-form-caps.spec.js`, 1 in `notices-pages.spec.js` and, for the card below, 1 in `harness.spec.js`: the 31 of section 3. The 22 cases in `notices-events.spec.js` call the event code directly with a recording transport, so they read `PASS`. The code also texts the customer when a job is started or finished from the provider's page (and e-mails a customer with no account when it is finished), two notices the contract does not list; they go through the same machinery and are attempts only in the same way.

**The card held, not charged** (W1). With no Stripe secret key, a booking made outside production is recorded as `pending`, never as `authorized`, and the completion cannot charge it (`booking-payment.spec.js`, "a booking made where no payment key is set is recorded as pending with no held amount"). The case that asks the payment-intent route for a hold (`harness.spec.js`) ends `ATTEMPTED`: with no key the route cannot start a payment and answers 500. What the suite does not show is Stripe accepting the hold.

**What would turn them into passes.** For the texts and the e-mail: the three Twilio variables and an SMTP account (section 5 names them), and the list of variable names that section 2 has you set in `E2E_APP_ENV_NAMES`. The suite learns which credentials the app holds only from that list: without it every credential counts as missing, and these cases stay `ATTEMPTED` whatever the app holds. The card is not as simple, and a Stripe test-mode secret key and publishable key do not just turn the hold into a pass. With a secret key in the app, the booking route wants a payment that was held for the signed-in customer and refuses a booking without one (400, "This payment does not match this booking."). The case "a booking made where no payment key is set is recorded as pending with no held amount" (`booking-payment.spec.js`) sends no sign-in, so with a key it would fail, and W1 could not then read as met. The case "approving on a server with no payment key is a 503 that records nothing" (`job-reports.spec.js`) skips itself once `STRIPE_SECRET_KEY` is in the list, and the acceptance command counts a skipped case that the map does not name as a gap against W8. The `stripe-complete` case in `ownership.spec.js` skips itself the same way, because it would reach Stripe, and the map takes that whole file for W12, so the acceptance command would then read W12, a hard gate, as NOT MET. With an SMTP name in the list, the valid sign-up case in `provider-signup-page.spec.js` skips itself too (the sign-up would mail an admin); no item of the contract rests on it. All of this is read from the cases; the suite has not been run in that state. Run it with test credentials before relying on it, expect these cases to need attention, and watch the first real sends of each kind in `notice_attempts`.

## 5. What you must supply before production

- **Stripe.** A live secret key (`STRIPE_SECRET_KEY`), the publishable key (`NEXT_PUBLIC_STRIPE_PUBLISHABLE_KEY`) and the signing secret for Stripe's webhook (`STRIPE_WEBHOOK_SECRET`, read by `/api/stripe/webhook`), and test-mode keys for a staging site. `STRIPE_COUNTRY` and `STRIPE_CURRENCY` are `CA` and `cad` in the example file. The webhook route acts on four kinds of event (a connected account updated, a transfer paid, a transfer failed, and the checkout payments for overtime and for a balance) and only logs three others (a transfer created, a payment succeeded, a charge captured), so the one secret guards all seven. It writes the whole request body and the request's headers to the application log before it checks the signature, and the first eight and last four characters of the signing secret as well. **Without a secret key** the payment step that the booking page takes first (`/api/payment/create-intent`) answers 500 ("Failed to initialize payment"), so a customer cannot book. The booking route itself, given a payment id of the right shape, refuses in a production deploy with a 503 ("Payments are not available right now. Please try again shortly.") and writes nothing, and so does the customer's approval of a finished job; that is intended. Outside production the booking route records such a booking as `pending` and it is never charged (the suite sends a made-up payment id). An admin's booking on a customer's behalf holds no payment and does not check for the key. The card page says when the card is charged ("Nothing is charged now. {price} is charged to this card when the job is done."); the card-saving terms and the consent text are yours and your lawyer's: the build supplies none.
- **Twilio.** `TWILIO_ACCOUNT_SID`, `TWILIO_AUTH_TOKEN` and `TWILIO_PHONE_NUMBER`, a number approved to text Canadian numbers, and a spend limit or usage alert in the Twilio account itself (no code replaces it). The code bounds what it spends: 8 texts a day to one number (2 for a quote request), 200 a day for one provider and 500 a day for the platform. A text goes only to a Canadian number (+1 with a Canadian area code). A cleaner is sent a job link only after the cleaner's number has passed a check: a six-digit code is texted to it and the provider enters the code the cleaner reads back. So the check needs Twilio too, and until it works no cleaner can be sent a link. In a production deploy `NEXT_PUBLIC_APP_URL` must be the real `https:` address of the site, with nothing after the host name: with anything else, unset included, the card link and the job link are not sent (`no_origin`). Make the first real sends one booking of your own.
- **E-mail.** `SMTP_HOST`, `SMTP_PORT`, `SMTP_USER`, `SMTP_PASS` and `EMAIL_FROM`, and `ADMIN_EMAIL`. Admins who are told are every admin user plus `ADMIN_EMAIL`, once each. Two older mails are the exception: the one about a provider's sign-up (name, e-mail and phone) and the one at the end of onboarding (name, e-mail and specialty) go only to `ADMIN_EMAIL`, and where it is unset to a personal address written into the code (`src/app/api/provider/signup/route.js` and `src/app/api/provider/onboarding/complete/route.js`), so set it before launch. Unset, the host is `smtp.gmail.com` and the port 587, and the connection is always opened in plain text and upgraded by STARTTLS when the server offers it, which is what port 587 expects; a mail provider that offers only port 465, which wants TLS from the first byte, will not connect without a change to the code. The code also takes `EMAIL_USER` and `EMAIL_PASS` where `SMTP_USER` and `SMTP_PASS` are unset, and sends from `EMAIL_FROM`, else from `SMTP_USER`, else from `WorkOnTap <noreply@workontap.com>`.
- **Secrets.** `JWT_SECRET` has no built-in value: with it unset every customer, provider and admin sign-in answers 500, every one of their sessions is refused and the mobile app is signed out when it next refreshes (a cleaner's sign-in by code at a job link does not use it). Changing it ends every customer, provider and admin web session. `CRON_SECRET` is needed by the three scheduled jobs in `vercel.json`: the hourly sweep that approves a finished job its customer has left for 24 hours and takes the payment, the daily clean-up of crew data and the daily clean-up of job photos; they refuse to run without it. The clean-up of crew data is the retention rule for cleaners: one removed from a crew is anonymised after 365 days, the positions stored with check-ins are cleared after 365 days, the crew of a provider who has asked for deletion is erased after 48 hours, and expired sign-in codes are deleted after 7 days. The same job deletes the records of attempted texts and e-mails (`notice_attempts`) after 180 days, and the text counters once their day is more than two days old. A fourth route, `/api/cron/notifications` (reminders to new providers after 1, 24 and 72 hours, and night-before reminders to customers and providers), is not in `vercel.json`: it runs only if the host is told to call it, with the same secret. `AI_GATEWAY_SECRET_KEY` (or `MCP_SECRET_KEY`) guards the routes under `/api/ai-gateway/v1` (services, service locations and SEO text); unset, they answer 500, and the placeholder in the example file, `generate-a-secure-random-token-here`, works as the key if it is copied as it stands. The database login falls back without a message to host `localhost`, user `root`, password `root123` and database `workontap_db` for each of `DB_HOST`, `DB_USER`, `DB_PASSWORD` and `DB_NAME` that is unset. `DB_PORT` is in the example file but the app does not read it: it connects on port 3306. The example file `.env.example` lists nineteen names (the database, sessions, Twilio, e-mail and the AI gateway); it does not list the Stripe keys or `CRON_SECRET`, which the code reads, nor the names in the next bullet.
- **Other names the code reads.** Neither this note nor the example file gives these, and the code does not say which of them a production site needs; each is read where its feature is used. `NEXT_PUBLIC_GOOGLE_MAPS_API_KEY` (without it the address box on a service page is replaced by the message "Google Maps API Key is missing"), `NEXT_PUBLIC_BASE_URL` (the sitemap, `robots.txt`, `llms.txt`, the SEO tags and the AI gateway's OpenAPI file; unset, the sitemap, `robots.txt`, the SEO tags and the OpenAPI file name `https://workontap.com`, so a staging site that leaves it unset publishes the live site's address), `NEXT_PUBLIC_API_URL` (put in front of photo addresses on two pages, empty by default), `NEXT_PUBLIC_ADMIN_EMAIL` (the address that is told of a dispute or of a payment that could not be taken, where `ADMIN_EMAIL` is unset), `NEXT_PUBLIC_GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID`, `GOOGLE_CLIENT_ID_ANDROID` and `APPLE_ID` (sign-in with Google and with Apple), `FIREBASE_PROJECT_ID`, `FIREBASE_CLIENT_EMAIL` and `FIREBASE_PRIVATE_KEY` (push notifications; a `google.json` file in the project root works in place of the last two), and `JWT_EXPIRES_IN` (how long a provider's sign-in token lasts, 7 days by default; every other sign-in has its lifetime written into the code: 7 days, and 24 hours for the admin's). The defaults of `APPLE_ID` and `FIREBASE_PROJECT_ID` name accounts of the earlier code. A `NEXT_PUBLIC_` value is fixed when the site is built, so set it before the build.
- **Where photos live.** A production store for photos is **not chosen**. The host (Vercel) has no persistent disk, so photo capture works only where there is a disk, as does every upload in the app. The choices are an object-storage vendor (a recurring cost and a credential) or the database. Photos from a job link are written and deleted by one file, `src/lib/job-photo-store.js`, and its header says a new store means rewriting that file and its address constant. The app keeps two kinds of photo, and they are named and kept differently. A photo taken from a job link has a random 128-bit name, which is its only protection: anyone who has the address can read it, and it may be cached for a year. A JPEG has its Exif block (the camera's position among it) taken out before it is stored, and the daily clean-up deletes the photo after 365 days, except while its booking is `disputed`, when it waits until the status moves on. A photo in a job report is kept as long and goes the same way (the file is deleted, the report's row stays). A photo taken on the provider's page, and every other file that goes through `/api/upload`, is named with the time in milliseconds and the name of the file it came from, which can be guessed; it is stored exactly as it was sent, Exif included, is readable by anyone who has the address, may be cached for a year and is kept until someone deletes it: no schedule does, and the ways to are an admin's delete and deleting the service whose picture it is. If a store is on another origin, the content security policy must name it in `img-src`, and the site has a policy only on the job link page and the card link page (`/job/*` and `/card/*`): the point holds for the photos shown on those two and for no other page. Nothing deletes a single link photo on request (section 6). An admin can delete a file that went through `/api/upload` with `DELETE /api/upload?url=`; the admin services page calls it for a service's picture, and no screen calls it for a job photo. The same route also accepts an SVG, and the site serves it inline as `image/svg+xml`, a type that can carry script (section 7).
- **Hosting.** The texts and e-mails of the nine kinds of notice (the seven in section 4, a job started and a job finished) are sent after the response is returned, by Next's `after()`, so the host must keep the process alive until they finish. Older sends in the app are different: the chat message, a provider's approval, the account-deletion receipt, the reminders of the notifications route and a provider's password-reset e-mail are waited for inside the request. Others are started and not waited for: the customer's and the mobile app's password-reset e-mails, the receipt and the provider's mail after an approval, the "Job Completed" e-mail, the payment-failed alert to the admin and the admin's mail about a provider's onboarding. A host that ends the process when the response goes may cut those e-mails short. The job link page and the card link page, whose addresses are credentials, run none of the site-wide scripts (including those pasted into the SEO settings); make sure nothing at the host or a proxy adds a script to `/job/*` or `/card/*`. Do this before the first link is texted. Confirm that the application server and the database agree on the time zone.
- **The database.** The changes are plain SQL in `database/migrations/`, applied by hand, in this order: `0001` service pricing items, `0002` payment intent unique, `0003` marketplace pushed at, `0004` visit series, `0005` quote requests, `0006` booking card links, `0007` provider crew, `0008` job links, `0009` job photos and timer, `0010` job reports, `0011` notice attempts. Each has a `.down.sql`. The live schema is not in the repository, and `database/schema.js` is not a copy of it (section 2 lists what it lacks): a database built from it differs from the live one, and the suite has not been run against such a database. The older script `database/add_missing_columns.mjs` adds 12 of the 29 missing columns to an existing database when it is run by hand (it was not run for this note), and nothing in the repository adds the other 17. Before `0002`, run the query in its header: if it returns rows, stop (two bookings share a payment). The headers of the down files for `0005` to `0011` say what each discards, so export it first, and those for `0006` to `0011` say to deploy code without the routes that use the tables before running them, because those routes fail once the tables are gone (for `0009` it is columns that go; for `0011` the notice code never throws, so a request still succeeds, but each notice logs an error and nothing is sent). `0008` down discards every job link, sign-in code and session, every check-in and its position, the whole record of who did what at a link, every text counter and every number check, so every cleaner would have to be checked again; `0009` down loses who took each photo, every check-out and every over-quote flag. Deploy the code after the migrations, with the Stripe key set, and then enter the pricing rules. A service's rule is its catalog price and duration plus rows of the table `service_pricing_items` (a price per unit, an option, a property type, the weekend, an extra); with no rows a service books at its catalog price and duration, and `0001` adds none. No page and no route writes that table, so the rows are entered in SQL. The development data holds invented figures marked `is_placeholder = 1`, which is the default for a new row; the app does not read the flag, and the client's own figures are rows with it set to 0. The percent off a booking that repeats is not in that table. It is three keys of `system_settings`, `recurring_discount_weekly_percent`, `recurring_discount_every_2_weeks_percent` and `recurring_discount_monthly_percent`, each 0 until it is set (a number from 0 to 100 with up to two decimals; any other value counts as 0). The admin settings page offers only the default commission, so an admin sets these by posting a `key` and a `value` to `/api/admin/settings`, or they are set in the table.
- **One question.** Does the table `user_addresses` exist in production? The address routes (`/api/user/addresses` and `/api/user/addresses/{id}`) answer 500 without it. The development database does not have it, and no file in the repository creates it: not `database/schema.js`, not `database/schema.sql`, not a migration. So a database built from the repository has no such table either, and the two cases that need it are skipped by name (section 7).

## 6. What is deliberately not built

The contract's own out-of-scope list: extension approvals, contractor staff logins, mobile app changes, a live dashboard, refund and dispute flows, double-charge protection, new revenue features. Double-charge protection is not a feature of its own, but the main charges and payouts are made under a key built from the booking (or the report), so a repeat of the same request returns the first and is not a second charge: the capture and the charge at the end of a job, the added-items charge, and the payout to the provider, whether it is made when the customer approves or by the hourly sweep. The money an admin moves when resolving a dispute, and the payout the Stripe webhook makes after an overtime payment, carry no such key.

Knowingly left, in plain words:

- A production store for photos (section 5); a control for an admin to delete one job photo, and a way for a customer to ask for one to be deleted.
- A way to correct a check-in made by mistake; hardening of the link's check-out and the timer.
- A page where a customer approves or disputes an added-items charge before it is made. No customer has one, with an account or without: the office's approval of a report charges the card the customer has on file, and the customer's page for a job approves or disputes the job itself. Notices the contract does not list: a text to the customer when an added-items charge is made or declined, a text to the customer when a cleaner starts the job from the link, and an alert to the admin when a job goes over its quote.
- Older code, outside this work: two routes (provider sign-up and the end of provider onboarding) fall back to a hard-coded personal address where `ADMIN_EMAIL` is unset, and the older HTML e-mail templates in `src/lib/email.js` escape none of the values they insert; a provider who has signed up and not been approved, or who has been suspended, can sign in once their e-mail is verified (a rejected provider is refused) and use the provider routes (only the two routes that claim a job refuse them, and the routes that read the provider's own row do not refuse by status either); the admin's Jobs list reads only the bookings of customers who have an account, the newest 50, so a booking made as a guest, or by an admin for someone with no account, is not in it, is in the queue only while it waits for a provider or an acceptance, and after that is found only among the provider's jobs on the Users page; the admin's route that updates a dispute (`PATCH /api/admin/disputes`) reads three names that it never takes from the request (`action`, `capture_amount` and `provider_amount`, which the dispute screen sends), so it stops with a 500 for every dispute that exists and the screen cannot resolve one (found by reading the code and not run; the contract leaves dispute flows out of scope); the public checkout writes the customer's contact to the application log; and the customer's job page and the older finished-job e-mail say that a job is approved automatically after 12 hours, where the hourly sweep waits 24 (the new finished-job text says 24), so a customer can be told two different times.
- Small edges in the notices, the link pages and the card links. On the quote form: nothing warns anyone when its daily share of quote texts or e-mails is used up (the `notice_attempts` table shows it, as outcomes `kind_cap` and `mail_cap`); one mailbox written as many aliases (`name+1@`, `name+2@`) draws up to 100 quote e-mails a day and uses up the day's confirmations for real customers, whose requests are still saved; the same trick also fills the form's hourly limit (30 saved requests an hour in all, and the code's own comment says it can shut real customers out for an hour), and while that limit holds a real customer is answered 429 and nothing is saved. Both need a product decision (a confirmation before the first e-mail, or a CAPTCHA). And an address with a label and a colon in front of it (`label:mailbox@host`) is accepted and slips past the per-address limit, where it should simply be refused.

## 7. Known gaps in the evidence

- **The provider's "Start Job" button (W5).** Decision asked of you: close the second door, or accept it in writing. Closing it means changing the provider's page to open the job link, where the provider may start the job, and making the time-tracking route refuse a start. That is a change to `src/`, so it means a full run of the suite again (see "The label" below), and it does more than the sentence shows. (a) The text to the customer that the job has started is sent only by the provider's route today; the job link sends none, so closing the door ends that text unless the link's start is given it. (b) The cases that pin the open door turn red (`job-timer.spec.js`, case AC2, and `acceptance-links.spec.js`, "W5.one-door"), and so do the cases that start a job through the provider's route to set it up (`price-of-record.spec.js`, `pricing-rules.spec.js`, `notices-routes.spec.js` and `ownership.spec.js`), until they start from the link. (c) A job link can only be made for a job with a cleaner on its crew, and at the link even the provider's start is refused until a cleaner is checked in, so a provider who works without a crew list could no longer start a job. Accepting it means filling in `accepted` on `W5.one-door` in `e2e/acceptance/contract.js` with `by`, `on` and `ref`, all three as text (who accepted, when, and where it is written down). Anything else, a plain sentence for example, makes the map invalid, and `npm run acceptance` then stops with exit code 3 and says why, without reading the run. Until one of those is done, `npm run acceptance` exits 1 on W5.
- **Texts, e-mails and the card hold** are attempt-only (section 4). The journey case that follows one booking from checkout to a finished job (`acceptance-journey.spec.js`, eleven steps) has its limits too: the texts and the alerts end `ATTEMPTED`; the booking's date is moved to yesterday by a database update so that the check-in window is open (the checkout books a future day); each cleaner's number is marked as checked by a database update, because the check itself needs a text; the code a cleaner was texted is found from its stored hash, because no text is ever read; and the approval of an added item is shown only as a refusal (503, nothing recorded) because the approval route needs a Stripe key. The charge after an approval is proved in process, with a stand-in for the card service, in `acceptance-seams.spec.js`.
- **Two cases skipped by name.** Two cases in `ownership.spec.js` (the owner's read of an address; another customer's address refused) cannot be observed because the development database has no `user_addresses` table. - **Three older findings, not fixed.** (a) `GET /api/provider/available-jobs/{id}` returns the street and the instructions of a job on the marketplace to a provider of any status (active, pending, inactive and suspended all got an answer), where the list of open jobs hides the street until the provider has the job. The cases that prove W12 pin that a provider cannot read another provider's assigned job; they do not pin what a provider, of any status, can read of an open job nobody has claimed. (b) `POST /api/provider/signup` is public and e-mails whatever address is typed, with the typed first name, and e-mails the admin on each signup; one signup per address is the only limit, so the form can be used to send mail to other people's addresses. (c) On a host that has a disk (section 5), `POST /api/upload` takes a file from any signed-in customer, provider or admin and checks only that the type the sender names starts with `image/` and that the file is under 10 MB; it does not look at the file, and it keeps the extension the sender gave it. A file called `something.svg` is stored as sent and served from the site's own address as `image/svg+xml`, with no `Content-Disposition` header and no content security policy (the route that serves `/uploads/` sets only the type of the extension and a year of caching). An SVG can carry script, and a browser runs it when the address is opened as a page, though not when the same file is shown as a picture; so a signed-in customer can put up a page that runs script as the site, for whoever is sent its address. Measured on the development environment with an SVG that holds an empty script: a customer's upload answered 200, the file came back as `image/svg+xml` with no `Content-Disposition` and no policy, and an admin's delete removed it again. Refusing SVG at the route, or serving every upload with `Content-Disposition: attachment`, would close it; either is a change to `src/`, so a full run of the suite again. All three were found by reading the code, and (a) and (c) were also measured on the development environment; none is one of the nine defects.
- **Four timing-sensitive cases.** Four cases in `job-timer.spec.js` compare the job's clock with a value worked out from the time that has passed, to a tolerance of a few seconds, across several requests: the countdown's wall-clock case ("AC3", 6 seconds); the first "AC4" case (an hour on, a fourth cleaner checks in and the next read must be 2700; a check-out must then read 5400; 15, 6 and 10 seconds); the second "AC4" case (a cleaner taken off the crew and the clock divided again, 5400; 10 seconds); and "AC6" (an hour over the quote, 3600; 15 seconds). The "AC5" case that adds up each cleaner's worked seconds is not one of them: it takes the seconds that have passed from the server's own clock in the answer, so the time the requests take cancels out. On a loaded machine, or on a development server that has not compiled a route yet, one request can take 2 to 17 seconds, and those seconds are counted too, so a right clock can read as a wrong one. "AC3" was seen to fail in a busy window and pass when run alone. The first "AC4" case failed in the first window that ran it (10820 seconds where 10800 were expected, tolerance 15), failed again alone in a window of its own while its routes were still cold (5381 where 5400 were expected, tolerance 10), and passed alone once those routes had been compiled; the extra seconds were the requests, not the clock, and the arithmetic itself is shown in process. The second "AC4" case and "AC6" passed in the window that ran them. In the complete run all four read PASS: "AC3" from a run of five cases made on their own, the first "AC4" case from its third run, made alone once its routes were compiled, and the other two from their window.
